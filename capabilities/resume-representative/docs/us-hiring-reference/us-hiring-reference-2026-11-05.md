## 01. Executive synthesis and scope

Section ID: USH-01. Evidence class: synthesis. Geography: United States. Time: 2010–2026, with separately labeled scenarios.

The central change in U.S. hiring since 2010 is not the invention of a single superior selection technology. It is the expansion of digital access, the movement of parts of recruitment into software-mediated workflows, repeated changes in labor-market conditions, and growing requirements to explain how employers screen, evaluate, and compensate people. A defensible hiring system still needs a clear account of the work, evidence relevant to that work, a consistent decision process, and an accountable employer. OPM's job-analysis guidance, EEOC's selection guidance, and modern selection research support those foundations rather than a technology-first model (OPM n.d.-a; EEOC 2007; Sackett et al. 2023).

The historical trajectory is uneven. National unemployment averaged 9.6 percent in 2010 and 5.3 percent in 2015; the seasonally adjusted monthly rate reached 3.5 percent in September 2019. Those statistics describe different temporal measures, not a single perfectly comparable annual series. They establish a broad shift in economic conditions, not proof that every employer gained or lost bargaining power at the same time (BLS 2011, 2016, 2019a). Hiring behavior must also be interpreted by occupation, location, employer size, and skill availability.

The pandemic changed where some people worked and how many employers conducted recruitment. Census data show that the share primarily working from home rose from 5.7 percent in 2019 to 17.9 percent in 2021. That measure does not mean that the same proportion had any hybrid arrangement, nor does it describe jobs that require a physical presence (Census Bureau 2022). The resulting managerial problem is to distinguish the capabilities needed to do the work from assumptions about where a successful worker ought to sit.

Technology adds operational choices, not exemptions from sound selection. The ability to parse a resume does not establish the validity of ranking it. A favorable candidate experience does not establish predictive accuracy. Removing a degree from a posting does not demonstrate that non-degree candidates are being hired. An algorithmic audit does not certify every aspect of a tool's legality or effectiveness. These distinctions recur throughout this reference because collapsing them creates avoidable business and human risk.

The recommended operating stance is evidence-based and proportional: automate low-risk administration where useful, validate consequential assessment methods, retain meaningful human accountability, and monitor outcomes rather than marketing claims. This is this report's synthesis, not a claim that all U.S. employers already operate this way. AI is a secondary component of the wider hiring system.

## 02. Evidence, chronology, and interpretation

Section ID: USH-02. Evidence class: methodology. Review date: 2026-11-05.

This reference uses a targeted narrative review, not a systematic review with a registered search protocol. Sources include federal statistical releases, agency guidance, enacted state legislation, primary professional surveys, product documentation, and selection research. The bibliography contains the sources actually used. Sources differ in evidentiary purpose: a statute establishes legal text; an agency FAQ explains an agency's interpretation; a vendor manual establishes a product's documented behavior; a survey describes its respondents; and a research study estimates relationships within its design.

Historical periods are non-overlapping calendar intervals: 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2024. The familiar five-year breakpoints of 2010, 2015, 2020, and 2025 remain visible, without counting a boundary year twice. January 2025 through November 5, 2026 is a partial current interval. No passage should be read as observing the rest of 2026 or 2027–2029. The original project's 2030 and 2035 outlook anchors are replaced in this edition by 2031 and 2036, approximately five and ten years beyond the current review year.

Four evidence labels govern interpretation. **Observed** means the cited source supports the stated observation. **Analysis** means the report draws a qualified conclusion from evidence. **Operating proposal** means a recommended design, not a measured national norm. **Scenario** means a conditional future possibility. Synthetic examples are not actual employers, candidates, salary surveys, or test-validation studies.

No universal national adoption rate is asserted for applicant tracking systems, structured interviewing, automated rejection, or skills-first selection. Where a source reports a survey percentage, the population and observation date matter more than the apparent precision of the number. SHRM's 2025 Talent Trends report, for example, draws on an unweighted panel of 2,040 U.S. HR professionals surveyed in February 2025; it is not a census of all employers (SHRM 2025).

Legal coverage is deliberately illustrative, not a fifty-state compliance inventory. A current implementation must check its actual jurisdiction, employer coverage, applicant location, statutory definitions, effective dates, regulations, and relevant orders. The review date is not a promise that every pending case or rulemaking has been found. The Illinois AI statute is included, but detailed implementing-rule status was not independently established for this edition. That limit must remain attached to any retrieved legal answer.

## 03. The hiring system as a sequence of decisions

Section ID: USH-03. Evidence class: operating proposal. Related sections: USH-08 through USH-20.

A useful business model separates seven decisions: whether work needs additional capacity; what work and outcomes are required; where suitable applicants may be found; which evidence to collect; how to evaluate that evidence; what employment proposition to offer; and how to verify the eventual result. The proposed model prevents a resume score from being mistaken for the entire hiring decision. It also distinguishes headcount approval from a candidate's qualifications.

The process begins with a business need, not a recycled job advertisement. A vacant position may represent a continuing role, a changed role, temporary work, or work that could be redistributed. The hiring manager should document expected outputs, constraints, decision authority, and the consequences of a poor appointment. HR should then translate that account into a reviewable requisition and assessment plan. This is an operating recommendation grounded in the job-analysis principle, not a statutory sequence (OPM n.d.-a).

Each transition should have an owner, evidence requirement, reason code, and exception route. Initial eligibility screening might establish a required license; an interview might examine judgment; a work sample might demonstrate a specific task. The evidence should not silently change purpose. A writing sample designed to assess clarity should not become an undisclosed personality test. Availability should not be inferred from family circumstances.

Candidate records should preserve both evidence and uncertainty. Useful states include verified requirement met, plausible evidence requiring confirmation, not yet assessed, and requirement not met. A missing statement is not equivalent to a demonstrated inability. Conversely, an impressive claim is not verification. The workflow should allow a narrow clarification when that would resolve a material uncertainty without imposing inconsistent burdens.

The hiring system ends with feedback from actual work, not the signed offer alone. Early onboarding, job design, supervision, resources, and training influence performance. A failed hire is therefore not automatically evidence that the candidate assessment failed. The organization needs a traceable chain from requirement to assessment to decision to subsequent outcome, while avoiding retrospective rewriting of what interviewers knew.

## 04. 2010–2014: recovery, digital access, and screening pressure

Section ID: USH-04. Evidence class: historical evidence and analysis. Historical interval: 2010-01-01 through 2014-12-31.

The opening period followed the Great Recession. BLS reported a 9.6 percent national annual unemployment rate for 2010. That establishes substantial national labor-market slack, but not a measured hiring policy for each firm (BLS 2011). A cautious interpretation is that many employers had access to more applicants relative to the recovery's available jobs, while specialized labor markets could still present shortages. Claims that all recruiters became more selective, or that a particular interview length was nationally standard, require separate evidence and are not asserted here.

The strongest documented transition is the growing importance of digital job search. A 2015 Pew survey looking back at recent searches found that 79 percent of Americans who had sought work in the preceding two years used online resources in their most recent search. The finding helps characterize the end of the 2010–2014 interval. It is an applicant-side measure, not an ATS adoption rate or evidence that networking became irrelevant (Smith 2015).

For resume evaluation, digital submission changed the medium through which employers encountered claims. A useful historical interpretation is that the application increasingly became both a document and a set of searchable fields. However, the available sources do not justify a universal account of how every ATS ranked applicants. The reference therefore preserves a distinction between the broad digital transition and employer-specific screening rules.

Selection science did not begin during this period. Job-related assessment, structured comparison, and concerns about discriminatory selection were already established before 2010. EEOC's 2007 guidance discusses tests, interviews, and selection procedures in relation to federal discrimination law. It should not be read as a new law enacted in 2010, but as part of the framework within which later technologies operated (EEOC 2007).

Compensation analysis for this period should likewise resist false precision. Annual wage data, occupational comparisons, internal job values, and employer affordability are different inputs. The report does not equate recovery-era wage growth with a universal merit increase. The durable lesson is methodological: attach a date, population, pay definition, and geography to compensation evidence rather than presenting one number as the salary for a job title.

Comparative interpretation: for resume review, the central transition was the medium of access rather than proof of a universal ranking algorithm. For interviews and assessments, established methods continued within increasingly digital application flows; the sources do not establish a national structured-interview adoption rate. For compensation, the key analytic requirement is period-specific market evidence rather than a presumed national raise. For HR, the enduring operational problem was managing applicants without confusing volume reduction with evidence quality. These comparisons are synthesis, not additional measured statistics.

## 05. 2015–2019: tighter markets and more visible employment propositions

Section ID: USH-05. Evidence class: historical evidence and analysis. Historical interval: 2015-01-01 through 2019-12-31.

The national annual unemployment rate was 5.3 percent in 2015; by September 2019 the seasonally adjusted monthly rate was 3.5 percent (BLS 2016, 2019a). These indicators support the broad conclusion that the labor market tightened relative to 2010. They do not prove that every occupation became candidate-driven. The practical implication is conditional: when qualified alternatives are scarce, delay, unclear requirements, and an unattractive offer become more costly to an employer.

Digital recruitment became a more explicit management discipline. LinkedIn's 2018 survey identified diversity, changes in interviewing, data, and AI as prominent themes among more than 8,800 talent professionals and hiring managers globally. The source records professional perceptions across countries, not verified U.S. implementation or causal gains from technology (LinkedIn 2018). This distinction matters because optimistic expectations about AI later became confused with evidence that tools reduced bias.

Pay practices also changed through jurisdiction-specific legislation. Massachusetts enacted its updated equal-pay law in 2016, with the relevant changes taking effect July 1, 2018. The law restricted salary-history inquiries, among other provisions. This is a concrete example of a shift away from treating a person's prior compensation as the default anchor for an offer, not proof that every state imposed the same restrictions at once (Massachusetts Attorney General n.d.).

Compensation-cost measures show another useful distinction. BLS reported that private-industry compensation costs increased 2.7 percent over the year to September 2019, with wages and salaries rising 3.0 percent and benefit costs 2.0 percent. Those are labor-cost measures, not a universal annual raise recommendation or a guarantee that a particular employee experienced the same increase (BLS 2019b).

The analytic trajectory is a widening employment proposition. Recruitment increasingly involved presenting compensation, opportunity, work arrangements, and organizational expectations alongside finding applicants. Nonetheless, stronger employer branding is not evidence of stronger selection validity. A polished careers page and an engaging interview can coexist with poorly defined evaluation criteria. The operating response is to connect attraction promises to the actual role and to assess applicants consistently after they enter the process.

Comparative interpretation: relative to the recovery period, the tighter national market increased the potential cost of a slow or unclear hiring process, although the effect remained role-specific. Resume and profile evidence could support both direct applications and proactive recruitment. Interview design faced the separate challenge of attracting candidates while evaluating them consistently. Pay-history restrictions in particular jurisdictions made the justification for an offer more explicit. None of these mechanisms establishes that all employers changed simultaneously or that informal evaluation disappeared.

## 06. 2020–2024: disruption, remote hiring, and renewed scrutiny

Section ID: USH-06. Evidence class: historical evidence and analysis. Historical interval: 2020-01-01 through 2024-12-31.

The pandemic produced a severe labor-market shock. The initial BLS release for April 2020 reported unemployment of 14.7 percent; this figure is explicitly the release vintage, not a claim about every subsequent statistical revision (BLS 2020). The sequence that followed was not one continuous environment. Employers experienced different combinations of closures, demand recovery, staffing shortages, turnover, and later cooling. A five-year label should not erase those changes or imply that a recruitment tactic effective in 2021 remained appropriate in 2024.

Remote work expanded sharply for jobs that could support it. Census measured the share primarily working from home at 5.7 percent in 2019 and 17.9 percent in 2021 (Census Bureau 2022). The operational inference is that a larger set of employers and applicants had reason to use remote recruitment, interviewing, and onboarding. The statistic itself does not measure the percentage of interviews held by video, and this reference does not manufacture such an adoption estimate.

Virtual delivery introduced a useful distinction between assessment content and delivery conditions. A candidate may be competent at the job but affected by bandwidth, an inaccessible platform, a noisy environment, or an unsuitable test interface. DOJ's 2022 AI and disability guidance explains how algorithmic tools can screen out qualified people with disabilities and emphasizes employers' responsibilities when using such tools (DOJ 2022). That concern extends the older requirement to assess the relevant capability rather than an incidental barrier.

Skills-first hiring also requires distinguishing announcements from outcomes. A 2024 matched-role study found that removing degree requirements often did not substantially change actual non-degree hiring. Its selected observational sample does not represent every employer (Sigelman, Fuller, and Martin 2024).

New York City's automated employment decision tool regime illustrates the regulatory response. Its law became effective in January 2023, with enforcement beginning July 5, 2023. Applicability depends on the law's definitions, not merely whether a product is marketed as AI (NYC DCWP n.d.). The period's durable trajectory is therefore digital scale accompanied by more explicit scrutiny of evidence, accessibility, and accountability.

Comparative interpretation: relative to 2015–2019, the pandemic made delivery conditions more visible in interviewing and assessment. Remote access could expand a pool while introducing technology and accommodation barriers. Skills-first announcements made the distinction between screening proxies and actual capability more salient. Compensation and location policies had to be explained when the workplace was no longer necessarily the recruiting market. These are operational implications of the cited developments, not estimates of universal practice.

## 07. 2025–2026: the current partial interval

Section ID: USH-07. Evidence class: current evidence. Observation cutoff: 2026-11-05. This is not a completed 2025–2029 period.

The most recent national unemployment observation specifically verified for this edition is September 2026: BLS reported 4.2 percent, with 7.1 million unemployed people. The release described the rate as little changed. These figures are not an estimate for November or a forecast of the next year (BLS 2026). For an actual hiring decision, a national headline remains a context measure; the relevant recruiting market may be a local trade, licensed profession, specialized technical role, or internal succession pool.

SHRM's 2025 survey reported that 43 percent of respondent organizations used AI in HR tasks, compared with 26 percent in its 2024 reporting. The survey is a useful observed milestone but not a current census and not proof of improved hiring outcomes. Its unweighted panel and broad HR scope constrain interpretation (SHRM 2025). This edition does not label that 2025 figure the latest possible AI adoption estimate in 2026.

The legal environment also changed. Federal contractor guidance predating January 2025 cannot be repeated without checking the rescission of Executive Order 11246 and DOL's consequent enforcement direction. Separate statutory duties are not erased simply because one executive-order framework changed (DOL 2025; OFCCP 2025). A renewed knowledge file must track what was rescinded, what remains, and what applies to the particular employer.

State AI rules demonstrate the cost of stale summaries. Illinois Public Act 103-0804 took effect January 1, 2026. Colorado's 2026 legislation repealed and reenacted the earlier AI provisions, with specified new requirements beginning January 1, 2027. As of this edition, the latter date is future, not evidence of an already operative requirement for all 2026 decisions (Illinois General Assembly 2024; Colorado General Assembly 2026; Colorado Attorney General n.d.).

The current operating problem is not simply whether to adopt AI. It is how to maintain a coherent hiring process when applicant content, tools, law, labor supply, and organizational expectations can change at different speeds. This report proposes version-controlled requirements, tool inventories, source-dated pay decisions, and explicit review triggers. Those are recommended controls rather than claims that a universal new HR standard has already emerged.

## 08. Job analysis and requirements design

Section ID: USH-08. Evidence class: operating proposal grounded in selection principles. Keywords: job analysis, essential functions, competency, minimum qualification.

Job analysis links tasks to the competencies needed to perform them. OPM describes it as a foundation for assessment and selection and as a basis for identifying job requirements (OPM n.d.-a). For business use, the important distinction is between the work itself, the outcomes expected from that work, and the proxies an employer has historically used to identify suitable people.

The following design is a proposal for a hiring brief. Begin with a small set of expected outcomes, such as producing accurate estimates, resolving customer cases, maintaining safe equipment, or delivering a governed system integration. For each outcome, record the tasks, constraints, stakeholders, and evidence that would demonstrate acceptable performance. Add the level of autonomy and the consequences of error. Avoid substituting a long technology list for an account of the work.

Classify requirements into essential at entry, learnable within the organization's actual training capacity, and preferred but nonessential. Required legal credentials belong in their own category because an employer cannot replace a legally necessary license with an attractive portfolio. Years of experience should be treated as an imperfect proxy unless a specific requirement makes duration relevant. A person may repeat a narrow task for years without developing the breadth the role needs.

Freeze a version of the requirements before reviewing applicants. A legitimate change should trigger a documented revision and a consistent reconsideration of affected candidates. Otherwise, the process can drift toward whichever individual the manager happens to prefer. A preferred attribute should not become an undisclosed elimination criterion midway through a search.

For a synthetic customer-support role, the essential requirements might be accurate issue identification, clear customer communication, appropriate escalation, and dependable use of the required workflow. A specific prior software brand may be learnable. A degree may be unnecessary unless supported by the work or another legitimate requirement. The example is not a validated job analysis; an employer must conduct its own work inquiry and verify legal and operational constraints.

A well-designed brief also states what the selection process will not infer: personality from appearance, commitment from family status, competence from accent, or honesty from a polished resume alone. That restraint is part of preserving the distinction between relevant evidence and unsupported interpretation.

## 09. Sourcing, internal mobility, and candidate access

Section ID: USH-09. Evidence class: operating proposal. Keywords: sourcing, referrals, internal candidates, access, employer proposition.

Sourcing is the design of access to a candidate pool, not the assessment of every member of that pool. Employers may use public postings, professional networks, referrals, agencies, educational partnerships, apprenticeships, internal vacancies, and direct outreach. This report does not rank those channels universally. The appropriate mix depends on the role, available labor, cost, accessibility, and the organization's development capacity.

The proposed control is to evaluate channels against comparable outcomes. A referral channel might produce a high interview rate because employees understand the role, because referred applicants receive preferential treatment, or both. A high conversion rate alone cannot distinguish these explanations. Track who entered, who was eligible, what evidence was gathered, and why applicants advanced. Keep channel membership separate from the capability score.

Internal mobility deserves its own treatment. Internal candidates may provide richer performance evidence, but that evidence is shaped by prior opportunity, manager support, and task allocation. An employee should not be evaluated solely on exposure they were never allowed to obtain. Conversely, familiarity with the company should not substitute for capability in a materially different role. Define how internal evidence maps to the new job and what additional assessment is needed.

Candidate access requires practical design. A short application may improve completion but collect insufficient evidence; a long one may duplicate the resume and impose avoidable burden. Use the minimum information required at each stage, explain what happens next, and offer an accessible route for accommodation requests. Digital access research from the mid-2010s is a reminder that online recruitment can widen reach while still excluding people who cannot use a particular process effectively (Smith 2015).

The employment proposition should be specific enough to support self-selection: responsibilities, location expectations, schedules, travel, pay information where applicable, and the assessment burden. Avoid presenting a temporary flexibility arrangement as a permanent contractual promise. Record who owns the accuracy of the posting and who can authorize a change. This sourcing proposal is intended to improve operational clarity; its effect on applicant quality must be measured rather than assumed.

## 10. Resume, application, and portfolio review

Section ID: USH-10. Evidence class: product evidence plus operating proposal. Keywords: ATS, parsing, screening, resume, portfolio.

An applicant tracking system can store applications, extract fields, route work, apply configured rules, or integrate assessments. Those functions are not interchangeable. Greenhouse's documentation, for example, explains that an unsuccessful resume parse can leave the document attached while fields require manual entry. Complex formatting can affect extraction, but a parse failure is not the same thing as automatic rejection (Greenhouse 2026). Product-specific documentation must not be generalized into a universal rule for every employer's system.

The proposed review workflow separates extraction, eligibility, evidence mapping, and verification. Extraction establishes what the candidate actually submitted. Eligibility checks a small set of legitimate requirements. Evidence mapping connects relevant achievements and responsibilities to the job brief. Verification identifies which material claims need corroboration through questions, work samples, credentials, or references. A single matching percentage conceals these different tasks.

Review resume claims at their proper level. A job title is context, not proof of scope. A technology mention establishes a claim of exposure, not necessarily independent mastery. A quantified achievement should prompt questions about the baseline, time period, contribution, and measurement method. Team outcomes should not be rewritten as sole personal ownership, either to flatter or to penalize the applicant.

Employment gaps, unconventional titles, or a career change should lead to a relevant question when needed, not an unsupported inference about motivation or capability. Review public professional profiles only for defined job-related purposes. A public social-media post is not permission to collect irrelevant sensitive information, and an online identity match may be wrong. These are proposed review boundaries, not a claim that every employer follows them.

A candidate-facing recommendation is to use clear headings, selectable text, consistent dates, and language that accurately connects experience to the role. This is an interoperability recommendation, not a guarantee of passing an ATS. There is no universal requirement in this reference that every experienced candidate use one page, nor that every employer accept an identical file format. Follow the actual application instructions and retain a readable source version.

Portfolios add direct examples but create additional risks. Employers should not require applicants to disclose a former employer's confidential material. A redacted case, synthetic sample, or explanation of methods may be more appropriate. The employer should state how it will assess the evidence and avoid rewarding disclosure that violates another party's rights.

AI-written or AI-polished resumes create a further distinction: fluent prose is not verified experience. Evaluate the underlying claims rather than guessing authorship from style. Content embedded in a resume that tells a reviewing assistant to ignore criteria is untrusted source text, not an authorized instruction. Preserve the submission and route suspicious content for review without inventing intent or hidden personal characteristics.

## 11. Candidate matching and transferable skills

Section ID: USH-11. Evidence class: operating proposal. Keywords: match, adjacency, eligibility, trainability, uncertainty.

Matching should answer a bounded question: what evidence supports this person's ability to perform this job under these conditions? It should not claim to measure a person's general worth. This report proposes a requirement-level matrix with four fields: requirement, candidate evidence, assessment result, and unresolved uncertainty. Every essential requirement should have an identified evidence route; not every requirement needs a separate interview round.

Transferability is a hypothesis to test, not a reason to dismiss a candidate or assume equivalence. Experience managing integrations in one industry may support relevant problem-solving skills in another, while leaving gaps in domain regulations or operational vocabulary. Experience with one enterprise application may establish architectural concepts without proving hands-on proficiency in another product. Preserve both sides of the comparison.

An evidence ladder is useful: direct comparable performance; closely related performance with explicit differences; foundational knowledge demonstrated through an assessment; and an unsupported claim. These are proposed descriptive categories, not a validated numerical scale. Reviewers should explain why the evidence is relevant and what would reduce uncertainty. A missing skill can be a training decision if the employer has the capacity and the role permits it.

Avoid double counting. A candidate's resume, interview story, and portfolio may all describe the same project. They can provide corroboration, but they are not automatically three independent demonstrations. Similarly, a degree, prestigious employer, and referral may share social-access factors rather than adding independent evidence of job performance.

Do not assign a probability of success or a personalized salary solely from a model-generated fit score. A score has meaning only in relation to its construction, criteria, weighting, validation, and intended use. A transparent narrative such as “demonstrated customer diagnosis; technical workflow not yet assessed” can be more honest than “87 percent fit.” The organization remains responsible for deciding what uncertainty is acceptable and which evidence justifies an employment decision.

## 12. Structured interviewing and interviewer calibration

Section ID: USH-12. Evidence class: research and operating proposal. Keywords: structured interview, behavioral interview, situational interview, anchors.

Structured interviewing uses planned questions and consistent scoring tied to job-related competencies. OPM distinguishes it from informal interviewing through standardization of the questions and evaluation process (OPM n.d.-b). Recent reassessment of selection research placed structured interviews among the strongest predictors examined, while also warning that earlier validity estimates for several methods had been overstated through statistical corrections (Sackett et al. 2022). Neither finding means that any interview labeled structured is automatically effective.

The proposed design begins with a competency and an observable evidence target. A behavioral question asks for a relevant past example; a situational question asks how a candidate would handle a defined problem. Both require scoring guidance. A question about resolving a disagreement should specify whether the assessment concerns evidence gathering, stakeholder communication, escalation, or decision quality. Otherwise, reviewers may reward confidence rather than the intended capability.

Use behavioral anchors that describe response quality. In a synthetic example, a weak response may omit the stakeholder's actual need; an adequate response may diagnose the problem and propose a plausible action; a strong response may also identify constraints, communicate tradeoffs, and verify the result. These anchors require role-specific testing and calibration. They are not a psychometric instrument supplied ready for deployment.

Interviewers should record evidence and an initial assessment before a group discussion. A debrief should reconcile disagreements by returning to the evidence, not by allowing the most senior person to set an unexplained consensus. Maintain a distinction between what the candidate said, what the interviewer observed, and what the interviewer inferred. “Did not provide an example of escalation” is more useful than “not leadership material.”

Unstructured conversation can help establish rapport and explain the role, but a free-form conversation is not equivalent to a scored assessment. Separate the mutual-information portion from evaluative questions so candidates know what is happening. Similarly, replace vague “culture fit” or “culture add” labels with observable work behaviors, such as constructive challenge, reliable handoffs, or respectful disagreement. Personal similarity to the team is not a job requirement merely because it feels comfortable. This is a proposed design boundary.

Standardization does not require robotic treatment. Explain the process, permit appropriate clarification, and apply an accommodation process. Record permitted variations so reviewers understand the conditions under which evidence was collected. Do not improvise different difficulty levels for applicants based on familiarity or affinity.

Interview duration and number of rounds should follow the evidence plan. Another conversation is justified when it resolves a consequential unanswered question, not because the organization routinely adds a final informal veto. Candidate questions also provide mutual understanding, but they should not become an undisclosed test of personal similarity to the hiring team.

## 13. Work samples and other assessments

Section ID: USH-13. Evidence class: research and operating proposal. Keywords: work sample, simulation, assessment validity, test burden.

Work samples ask candidates to perform tasks resembling the work. OPM identifies their job-related strengths and also their development and administration costs. It cautions that a sample is most appropriate when the assessed competency is expected at entry rather than something the employer plans to teach after selection (OPM n.d.-c). The practical lesson is to align an assessment with the role's actual readiness requirement.

A synthetic analyst sample could provide a small, fictional dataset and ask for an explanation of an operational anomaly. A synthetic customer-service sample could ask the candidate to handle a realistic complaint. A synthetic operations sample could test sequencing and hazard recognition in a safe simulation. None should require access to confidential customer material or produce uncompensated commercial deliverables disguised as testing.

State the purpose, time expectation, permitted tools, evaluation criteria, and accessibility route before the assessment. When AI assistance is relevant to the real work, define whether the task tests independent knowledge, responsible tool use, or both. A tool-permitted task can assess verification and error detection; a tool-restricted task can assess an entry-level foundation. The two should not be silently mixed.

Job-knowledge tests, cognitive measures, situational judgment tests, personality instruments, and simulations measure different constructs. Their appropriateness depends on the job, evidence of validity, administration, reliability, and consequences. A research coefficient is not a warranty for a particular employer or applicant population. Modern selection-system research emphasizes the consequences of revised validity estimates and the need to reconsider combinations of methods rather than repeating an inherited ranking (Sackett et al. 2023).

The proposed assessment inventory records the intended construct, developer, version, job linkage, scoring rule, validation evidence, accommodations, data collected, retention, and appeal route. It also records what the assessment cannot establish. A coding exercise does not establish every aspect of engineering leadership; a personality questionnaire does not diagnose mental health; a fluent presentation does not prove the candidate originated the work.

Pilot an assessment before making it a high-stakes gate. Review completion patterns, scorer agreement, failure modes, and whether the task unfairly rewards prior exposure to a particular testing format. This pilot is developmental evidence. It does not replace the legal and professional evaluation required for consequential use.

## 14. Skills-first hiring and development pathways

Section ID: USH-14. Evidence class: research and operating proposal. Keywords: skills-first, degree requirements, apprenticeships, internal mobility.

Skills-first hiring means evaluating capabilities needed for work rather than relying unnecessarily on proxies. It does not mean that education, professional licensing, or accumulated experience never matter. The relevant question is whether a requirement serves a defensible function for this role. A regulated credential and an inherited preference for a particular institution are materially different.

The 2024 Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School analysis covered more than 11,000 matched roles in a selected large-employer dataset. Its evidence concerns observed changes after degree removal, not a randomized intervention or every U.S. employer (Sigelman, Fuller, and Martin 2024).

A proposed skills-first conversion therefore has several connected steps: revise the job analysis, change the application criteria, train reviewers, redesign assessments, update compensation and progression assumptions, and measure who actually advances and succeeds. Removing a degree phrase while leaving the same informal pedigree preferences intact is not a completed operating change.

Development pathways require real capacity. An employer should specify the training available, expected learning period, coaching ownership, and performance support. Hiring a promising learner into an unsupported role can produce failure that is later misattributed to the candidate's background. The alternative to a rigid experience requirement is not wishful thinking; it is a deliberate investment with an accountable owner.

Digital credentials and short courses may provide useful evidence, but their meaning varies. Verify what the credential assesses, who issued it, whether identity and performance were checked, and whether the capability is current. A badge inventory is not equivalent to demonstrated job performance. For candidates, the strongest narrative connects learning to a task, a result, and an appropriate example rather than simply accumulating course names.

## 15. Combining evidence and reaching a decision

Section ID: USH-15. Evidence class: operating proposal. Keywords: decision rule, evidence combination, debrief, uncertainty.

A hiring decision combines evidence under constraints. This reference proposes deciding the combination rule before reviewing the final slate. Some requirements are genuine gates; others permit tradeoffs; some are developmental. A mandatory license may be a gate, while two alternative technical backgrounds may both support the same capability. Treating all criteria as additive points can allow an impressive but irrelevant strength to conceal a critical unmet requirement.

The decision record should identify the role version, evidence reviewed, assessment conditions, significant uncertainties, comparison rationale, and final accountable decision-maker. It should explain why the chosen candidate meets the work requirements without narrating unsupported personal judgments about others. Rejected alternatives can be recorded narrowly and respectfully.

A panel should not use averaging to conceal disagreement about what is being measured. If one interviewer scored communication and another scored domain knowledge under the same heading, averaging their numbers does not create a meaningful composite. Resolve the construct, then evaluate the evidence. Missing assessment results should not be entered as zero, and an accommodation should not be treated as a negative score.

The proposed escalation rule is to seek additional evidence only when it could change a consequential decision. Examples include verification of a required credential, clarification of project ownership, or a targeted follow-up on a safety-critical task. Repeating broad interviews until everyone feels comfortable adds burden without necessarily reducing uncertainty.

A no-hire decision can be legitimate when no applicant meets a defensible requirement. It can also signal an unrealistic job brief, an uncompetitive offer, inaccessible sourcing, or a process that screens out trainable candidates. Reopen the business assumptions rather than assuming that every unfilled vacancy proves a talent shortage. This is an analytic diagnostic, not a presumption that any particular employer's criteria are unlawful.

Document the final decision before subsequent performance results are known. Later evaluation should compare the original expectations with actual outcomes, including the effect of onboarding and management. Retrospective rewriting destroys the evidence needed to improve the process.

## 16. References, background checks, and conditional offers

Section ID: USH-16. Evidence class: federal guidance and operating proposal. Keywords: references, FCRA, background reports, adverse action.

Reference checks and background reports serve different purposes. A reference conversation can clarify responsibilities or behavior; a credential check can establish a claimed qualification; a consumer report may trigger specific legal requirements. The employer should define what it needs to verify and why before collecting additional information. More personal data does not automatically produce a better decision.

FTC and EEOC guidance describes requirements associated with third-party employment background reports under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, including disclosure and authorization, provision of the report and rights information before adverse action, and a later adverse-action notice when applicable. State and local rules can add restrictions (FTC and EEOC 2014). This reference is not a substitute for the employer's current notices, procedures, or jurisdiction-specific legal review.

The proposed reference process uses consistent, job-relevant questions and separates firsthand knowledge from opinion. Ask about the work relationship, responsibilities, specific examples, and limits of the source's knowledge. Do not infer that a neutral reference policy establishes poor performance. Do not seek medical, family, or other irrelevant sensitive information as a shortcut for assessing reliability.

Background information requires context and accuracy. Name mismatches, incomplete records, and disputed information should not be collapsed into a categorical judgment. Maintain the applicable opportunity to review or dispute information and the appropriate decision timing. A software status such as “consider” is not itself the employer's documented rationale.

Conditional offers should clearly identify legitimate remaining conditions and the person responsible for resolving them. Medical inquiries and examinations require particular care because timing and consistency matter under disability law (EEOC 2007). Employment authorization verification, occupational licenses, safety requirements, and contractual terms each have their own rules; do not derive a universal deadline from this general reference.

Finally, retain only what is necessary under the applicable recordkeeping and litigation-hold requirements, with appropriate access controls. This document does not prescribe a single national retention period for every applicant record or employer category. Older guidance mentioning federal-contractor duties must be checked against later legal changes rather than copied mechanically.

## 17. Salary standards and market pricing

Section ID: USH-17. Evidence class: statistical methodology and operating proposal. Keywords: salary benchmark, job architecture, ECI, OEWS, compa-ratio.

Salary setting should distinguish the value of the job, the relevant labor market, internal equity, the candidate's supported qualifications, and the organization's compensation philosophy. A title alone is an unreliable matching key. A senior analyst in one company may manage a materially different scope from a similarly titled role elsewhere. Define job family, level, autonomy, decision impact, and location before selecting a market comparator.

BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics provide occupational wage estimates, not individualized offers. Their wage definition is not identical to total rewards or pure base salary: it excludes items such as employer benefit costs and overtime premiums while including certain other cash components. The program also has population and occupational-classification boundaries (BLS n.d.-a). Record those definitions before comparing OEWS data with a commercial salary survey or a posted range.

The Employment Cost Index measures changes in employer labor costs using a framework intended to control for occupational and industry composition. It is not a consumer price index, a guarantee of wage growth for a specific person, or a substitute for current job matching (BLS n.d.-b). When aging a survey, identify the index, period, relevant pay component, and method rather than applying an arbitrary inflation factor.

A proposed pricing workflow starts with several credible comparisons, reconciles job scope, normalizes annual versus hourly units, separates base from incentive and benefits, and records effective dates. Sources should not be averaged merely because they all contain a number. A small employer survey, a national occupational median, and advertised maximums may describe substantially different populations.

**Synthetic calculation:** assume a job's adopted midpoint is $100,000 and the proposed base salary is $95,000. The compa-ratio is 95,000 / 100,000 = 0.95. Assume an illustrative range of $80,000 to $120,000; range penetration is (95,000 − 80,000) / (120,000 − 80,000) = 37.5 percent. These measures describe position within a chosen structure. They do not establish that the structure is market-valid, equitable, or legally compliant.

A salary range should be supported by a coherent job architecture and approval process. Record why a candidate is placed at a particular point, what evidence supports an exception, and whether the decision creates compression or inversion with comparable employees. Do not optimize the offer solely to the lowest amount a candidate will accept. That recommendation is an equity and workforce-management judgment, not a quantified guarantee of retention.

## 18. Pay transparency, equity, and negotiation

Section ID: USH-18. Evidence class: jurisdiction-specific law and operating proposal. Review date: 2026-11-05.

Pay transparency is not one uniform national rule. California's Labor Commissioner guidance describes posting obligations for employers with at least 15 employees, subject to its coverage interpretation, and includes positions that may be filled in California, including remotely. It also explains salary-history restrictions and pay-scale provisions. Verify the current FAQ and statute for the actual employer and role rather than treating “remote” as a jurisdiction-free category (California DIR n.d.).

New York's guidance describes pay-range requirements for covered employers with four or more employees and for covered advertised opportunities, including promotion and transfer opportunities. Massachusetts General Laws chapter 149, section 105F applies a different threshold and became effective October 29, 2025. Its covered-employer threshold is 25 or more employees in the Commonwealth (New York DOL n.d.; Massachusetts General Court 2024). These examples demonstrate why a nationwide recruiter needs a coverage matrix rather than one memorized threshold.

Pay discussion rights are also distinct from job-posting requirements. The NLRB explains that employees covered by the National Labor Relations Act have rights to discuss wages. Coverage limits matter; the statement should not be transformed into an assertion that every worker is covered by the NLRA (NLRB n.d.). A historical employer preference for secrecy is not proof that restricting protected wage discussions was lawful.

The proposed compensation conversation begins by explaining the role's range, components, location policy, and placement criteria. Ask about expectations where lawful, rather than defaulting to prior pay as the job's value. Clarify base salary, target incentive, equity, benefits, scheduling, travel, and contingencies. A target bonus is not guaranteed cash, and a broad equity valuation is not equivalent to salary.

Transparency can expose pre-existing inconsistencies. An employer should examine current employees' pay when a new-hire market adjustment creates compression, while avoiding an automatic assumption that every difference is unlawful. Investigate relevant job scope and other legitimate factors. The purpose of the review is to understand and justify the structure, not to manufacture a post hoc explanation for any observed disparity.

This edition does not claim that transparency has eliminated negotiation, closed all demographic pay gaps, or produced a measured national participation effect. Those are empirical questions requiring suitable research. The practical recommendation is narrower: use accurate ranges and consistent explanations to reduce avoidable uncertainty and improve the auditability of offers.

## 19. Expectations, candidate experience, and onboarding

Section ID: USH-19. Evidence class: operating proposal. Keywords: expectations, candidate experience, realistic job preview, onboarding.

Candidate experience concerns whether the process is understandable, accessible, proportionate, and respectful. It is not a substitute for rigorous evaluation. An employer can explain a difficult assessment clearly, and a pleasant interview can still be an unreliable test. The proposed standard is to communicate the stages, approximate burden, permitted tools, decision timing, accommodation route, and contact for process problems.

Work arrangements should be described in operational terms. “Hybrid” may mean a fixed schedule, manager discretion, periodic travel, or attendance at particular events. Candidates and hiring managers need the same definition. State which expectations are contractual, policy-based, or subject to change. Do not infer that a candidate who asks about flexibility lacks commitment, or that an on-site role automatically requires unrestricted scheduling availability.

A realistic job preview should include ordinary constraints and tradeoffs, not only the appealing work. Examples include seasonal workload, customer escalation, repetitive tasks, documentation obligations, travel, or safety procedures. The point is informed agreement. This report does not prescribe disclosure of confidential strategy or invite an employer to make promises it cannot honor.

Onboarding should reuse the role's expected outcomes while distinguishing entry requirements from development goals. A proposed 30/60/90-day plan can define orientation, supervised execution, and increasing independence, but the timeline must fit the job. The plan should identify resources, coaching, feedback, and dependencies. A newly hired employee cannot fairly be evaluated on a delivery commitment that depends on access or decisions the organization has not supplied.

For measurement, keep process satisfaction, offer acceptance, readiness, performance, and retention separate. A declined offer may reflect compensation, timing, another opportunity, work arrangements, or personal factors that are not visible to the employer. Record candidate-reported reasons as reports, not proven causal explanations. Do not pressure applicants to disclose sensitive circumstances in order to explain withdrawal.

The hiring-to-onboarding handoff is an accountability boundary. Preserve the agreed role, compensation terms, outstanding conditions, and development commitments. Avoid transferring private interview details that do not serve a legitimate employment purpose. The goal is continuity of relevant evidence, not maximal circulation of applicant data.

## 20. Hiring metrics and evaluation design

Section ID: USH-20. Evidence class: original analytical framework. Keywords: funnel, conversion, quality of hire, validity, cohort.

Hiring metrics are useful only when their definitions are stable. Time to fill may begin at requisition approval, posting, or another event; time to hire may begin when a selected candidate entered the process. These are proposed distinctions to settle locally, not universal definitions enforced by this reference. Publish the event definitions and exclusions before comparing teams.

Track stage-specific conversion with the correct denominator. **Synthetic example:** 200 completed applications yield 40 qualified screens, 20 interviews, 8 offers, and 6 acceptances. Screen qualification is 40/200 = 20 percent. Interview-to-offer conversion is 8/20 = 40 percent. Offer acceptance is 6/8 = 75 percent. Reporting 6/200 = 3 percent as offer acceptance would be a denominator error.

Quality of hire requires more care than a single dashboard score. Early performance, retention, time to independent work, manager assessment, and employee experience measure different things. Retention can reflect a weak labor market rather than a successful match; manager ratings can contain bias; performance can depend on onboarding and opportunity. Define the construct, observation period, missing-data policy, and reason for each component before combining them.

Cohorts should be compared at equivalent maturity. A three-month-old hire cannot have a measured twelve-month retention outcome. Do not enter unavailable outcomes as failures or successes. Separate voluntary departure, involuntary departure, internal transfer, and organizational restructuring when those distinctions matter to the question.

Fairness monitoring should examine stage outcomes and appropriate comparison groups under qualified guidance. Small counts, changing applicant pools, and multiple comparisons can make raw percentages misleading. A simple ratio is a diagnostic signal, not a full legal or statistical determination. Do not infer protected characteristics from names, photographs, or language to fill a missing demographic field.

For process changes, use a design proportionate to the claim. A before-and-after improvement can be confounded by role mix, compensation, labor-market changes, or recruiting volume. A controlled pilot may provide stronger evidence, but employment consequences and fairness must be considered before experimentation. Record what changed, which population was exposed, and what alternative explanations remain. Claims of causation should be narrower than claims of association unless the design supports more.

## 21. Legal and accessibility boundaries

Section ID: USH-21. Evidence class: legal-source synthesis. Jurisdiction: U.S. federal baseline with selected state/local examples. Not legal advice.

Federal employment laws apply through distinct legal frameworks. EEOC's guidance explains that tests and other selection procedures can raise discrimination issues and that job relatedness, business necessity, accommodation, and prohibited inquiries matter. It also makes clear that using a vendor does not remove the employer's responsibility for its selection procedures (EEOC 2007). The details differ by law and factual setting; one generic “fairness score” cannot stand in for all of them.

Disability accessibility deserves explicit process design. DOJ's guidance describes ways hiring technologies can screen out qualified applicants with disabilities, including tools that measure incidental characteristics rather than the ability to perform the job. The recommended response is to examine accessibility and provide appropriate alternatives and accommodations, not to infer disability from assessment behavior (DOJ 2022).

New York City's covered automated employment decision tools require an independent bias audit within the specified period and related public information and candidate notices. The city describes a ten-business-day notice requirement for covered individuals and distinguishes the effective date from the July 2023 enforcement start. Applicability is definition-dependent (NYC DCWP n.d.). A city audit is not a guarantee of predictive validity, universal accessibility, or compliance in other jurisdictions.

Illinois Public Act 103-0804 amended the Illinois Human Rights Act concerning AI in employment, including discriminatory effects and use of ZIP codes as proxies for protected classes, effective January 1, 2026. Its notice provision uses statutory language that must be read carefully; this reference does not expand the wording or certify the status of implementing rules (Illinois General Assembly 2024).

Colorado SB26-189 became law in May 2026 and replaced the earlier framework. The legislature's enacted summary describes new developer documentation, deployer notice, correction, and human-review provisions, with specified requirements starting January 1, 2027. The attorney general's rulemaking page is a maintenance source for subsequent developments (Colorado General Assembly 2026; Colorado Attorney General n.d.). In this edition those future obligations are labeled enacted-but-not-yet-operative as applicable, not current nationwide law.

Finally, DOL's January 2025 direction concerning rescinded Executive Order 11246 changes how older federal-contractor materials should be used. OFCCP separately identified statutory authorities that were not eliminated by the executive-order rescission (DOL 2025; OFCCP 2025). Check actual current duties rather than claiming either that every earlier contractor requirement remains unchanged or that all equal-opportunity obligations disappeared.

## 22. AI in hiring: bounded use and accountable decisions

Section ID: USH-22. Evidence class: operating proposal with governance references. Keywords: AI, automation, human review, vendor assurance.

AI use should be classified by consequence, not by the presence of a fashionable label. Drafting a scheduling message, summarizing job-related evidence, recommending candidates, and making an adverse employment decision are different uses. This report proposes a risk-tiered inventory that records the intended function, affected people, inputs, outputs, user authority, data handling, and potential consequences.

Administrative uses can still fail. A scheduling assistant may disclose candidate information to the wrong recipient; a summarizer may omit a qualifying statement; a job-description generator may invent requirements. Require review appropriate to the action. A human approval button is not meaningful oversight when the reviewer cannot inspect the evidence, understand the output, or change the result.

For consequential assessments, ask the vendor what construct is measured, what validation supports the intended use, which populations and jobs were studied, what changes trigger revalidation, and how errors can be challenged. Request evidence rather than a general assurance that the system is unbiased. A tool trained on past successful hires may reproduce past opportunity patterns, and predictive performance on one population may not transfer automatically to another.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework is a voluntary framework for managing AI risks; it is not an employment-law certification. Its governance orientation can support a documented lifecycle approach, including attention to context, measurement, and management (NIST n.d.). The operating recommendations here adapt that general stance to hiring without claiming that adopting a checklist satisfies every legal obligation.

Avoid unsupported inference from faces, voices, emotion labels, or supposed personality signals. An employer should not treat an attractive automated description as evidence that the underlying construct has been validly measured. For generative tools, retain the distinction between source evidence and generated interpretation. A model must not invent a candidate achievement, credential, adverse fact, or reference statement.

The proposed minimum control set includes a named owner, approved use cases, access restrictions, source-linked outputs, version tracking, exception handling, incident response, and periodic outcome review. Thresholds should reflect consequence and evidence. Where a system cannot produce an adequately reviewable basis for a high-stakes recommendation, restrict or suspend that use rather than calling opacity an unavoidable feature of progress.

## 23. HR roles and the changing operating model

Section ID: USH-23. Evidence class: analysis and operating proposal. Keywords: HR, recruiter, hiring manager, compensation, governance.

AI and workflow automation can redistribute tasks within HR, but task redistribution is not the same as eliminating professional accountability. The proposed operating model separates business ownership of the role, recruitment operations, assessment design, compensation, legal/privacy review, and technology assurance. In a smaller organization one person may perform several functions, but the responsibilities should still be explicit.

Recruiters need to interpret evidence and diagnose the search, not merely move applicants through stages. That includes testing whether requirements are unnecessarily restrictive, explaining the actual employment proposition, coordinating appropriate assessments, and identifying where a process is losing suitable applicants. Automation may reduce some administration while increasing the need to inspect tool outputs and resolve exceptions.

Hiring managers remain responsible for defining the work and making a supported decision within organizational policy. They should not delegate the definition of success to an ATS ranking or use a late informal conversation to override documented criteria without explanation. HR should be able to challenge a requirement or decision when its basis is unclear.

Compensation specialists need reliable job architecture, appropriate market comparisons, and an explanation of range placement. People analysts need measurement definitions, data quality controls, and an understanding of causal limits. Legal, privacy, accessibility, and industrial-organizational expertise become particularly important when tools create consequential scores or when processes operate across jurisdictions.

This is a capability model, not a prediction that every organization will create new job titles. A small employer may obtain specialist support externally; a large employer may maintain dedicated assessment or assurance teams. The essential requirement is that ownership remains clear when a vendor changes a model, a regulation changes, or an applicant challenges an outcome.

Training should include practical error recognition. Staff should be able to distinguish an extracted fact from a generated inference, a survey from a population estimate, a current law from a proposal, and an operational metric from proof of effectiveness. The report's central HR expectation is disciplined judgment under changing conditions, supported by technology rather than replaced by it.

## 24. Sector, location, and employment-model differences

Section ID: USH-24. Evidence class: analysis and operating proposal. Keywords: sectors, hourly hiring, public employment, contingent work.

A cross-industry reference must not imply that all jobs resemble remote office work. Hourly operations may prioritize reliable scheduling, safe execution, and accessible application channels. Regulated professions may have legally required credentials. Senior leadership roles may require broader evidence of judgment and organizational impact. Public-sector and collectively bargained contexts may have additional rules that a private employer's discretionary process does not share.

These differences should change the assessment design, not justify irrelevant shortcuts. A safety-sensitive role may need a realistic assessment of safety judgment, but that does not authorize speculation about medical conditions. A licensed role needs credential verification, but a license alone may not establish every capability required by the job. An executive search may involve references and track-record review, but confidentiality should not become an excuse for an undocumented decision.

Geography affects the available labor market, applicable law, work arrangement, and compensation policy. A remote role can broaden reach while creating additional location-specific obligations. A multi-location employer needs a rule for which compensation market it uses and a separate process for legal coverage. Those are related decisions, not interchangeable ones.

The contingent workforce also requires precise definitions. BLS reported that independent contractors represented 7.4 percent of employment in their sole or main job in July 2023. That measure differs from estimates that count anyone doing occasional freelance work or earning platform income. BLS also separately measures contingent work, which is not identical to independent contracting (BLS 2024). Do not present a broad gig-economy headline as the share of employees hired as contractors.

This reference concerns employee hiring primarily. Contractor classification, procurement, and cross-border employment require separate legal and operational review. An organization's desire for flexibility does not determine a worker's legal status. The proposed workforce-planning comparison may consider employment, internal development, or outside services, but it must not turn classification into a cost-saving label selected without regard to actual work arrangements.

## 25. Projection point 2031

Section ID: USH-25. Evidence class: scenario, not forecast fact. Horizon: approximately five years beyond the review year.

**Baseline scenario:** by 2031, more employers could use integrated systems to organize job requirements, candidate evidence, assessment results, and compensation decisions. The expected change would be greater connectivity and traceability, not universal autonomous hiring. This is an inference from present digital workflows, documented AI use, and the direction of selected regulatory requirements, not a quantified adoption prediction (SHRM 2025; Colorado General Assembly 2026).

In this scenario, resume review becomes more explicitly evidence-oriented. Applicants may provide structured histories, portfolios, or credentials alongside conventional resumes. Interviews may concentrate on judgment, clarification, and demonstrated work because polished written applications become less discriminating as evidence. This is a plausible response to improved content-generation tools; the extent and effectiveness must be tested rather than presumed.

**Accelerated scenario:** interoperable credentials, affordable assessment design, and stronger outcome measurement could make skills-first hiring more substantive. Employers with development capacity might expand entry pathways and internal mobility. The necessary condition is organizational implementation, not merely a better matching algorithm. Watch actual outcomes, not announcements; see the evidence limitations in USH-14.

**Constrained scenario:** fragmented law, weak tool evidence, security incidents, inaccessible assessments, and applicant distrust could limit consequential automation. Organizations might retain digital administration while deliberately reducing automated ranking or restoring more direct work verification. A constrained scenario is not technological failure; it can be a rational response when assurance costs exceed demonstrated benefits.

The useful watch indicators are measurable: the share of roles with documented assessment plans; actual non-degree hiring into roles with changed requirements; assessment completion and accommodation outcomes; error and appeal rates; quality and availability of vendor validation; and the consistency of advertised and accepted compensation. No single indicator proves the entire scenario.

Disconfirming evidence should be recorded. If automation increases administrative speed without improving downstream evidence or outcomes, the baseline needs revision. If skills-first hiring changes only postings, the accelerated scenario has not occurred. If legal harmonization reduces uncertainty, the constrained scenario may become less likely. This edition assigns no numerical probabilities because no calibrated forecasting model was developed.

## 26. Projection point 2036

Section ID: USH-26. Evidence class: scenario, not forecast fact. Horizon: approximately ten years beyond the review year.

At a ten-year horizon, specific products and job titles are less defensible predictions than organizational choices. One plausible scenario is a more continuous talent system in which external hiring, internal movement, learning, and project staffing share evidence about capabilities. Such a system could reduce repeated data entry and make development pathways more visible. It could also concentrate sensitive information and amplify errors if access, correction, and purpose limits are weak.

A second scenario is deliberate pluralism. Organizations may use several hiring models at once: rapid entry pathways for trainable work, rigorous credential gates for regulated roles, work-based assessment for specialists, and deeper reference and judgment evaluation for executives. Technology could support those differences rather than forcing every applicant through one standardized algorithm.

Compensation could become more transparent and systematically linked to job architecture, while still differing by market, work arrangement, sector, and organizational policy. A federal harmonization of posting or AI rules is possible, but this reference does not predict that it will occur. State and local variation may remain significant. Equal work, market pricing, legal equal-pay standards, and individualized total rewards are not identical concepts and should not be collapsed into one future slogan.

The HR function could place more emphasis on assurance: verifying whether a selection process measures what it claims, whether people can understand and challenge errors, and whether automation changes opportunity as well as cost. This does not establish that recruiter headcount will rise or fall nationally. Task productivity, demand, organizational design, and the distribution of responsibilities jointly influence employment effects.

Important downside scenarios include overreliance on unverifiable credentials, automated amplification of historical disadvantage, excessive surveillance, and reduced access for candidates who cannot produce polished digital evidence. An opposite risk is refusing useful tools even where they could reduce repetitive burden without increasing consequential risk. The strategic question is not maximum or minimum AI; it is which decisions benefit from which evidence and controls.

The 2036 scenario should be renewed annually, preserving the original version and recording what changed. Watch actual mobility, training investment, assessment quality, accessibility, compensation structures, and dispute outcomes. Do not turn a prior forecast into a historical fact merely because its target year is approaching.

## 27. Three illustrative operating cases

Section ID: USH-27. Evidence class: synthetic examples and original analysis. No actual employer or applicant is represented.

### Case A: a customer-operations vacancy

A fictional employer needs an employee who can diagnose customer requests, use a case-management workflow, communicate clearly, and escalate appropriately. Its old posting requires a bachelor's degree and five years in the identical software. The proposed redesign first asks which requirements are essential at entry. It then uses a short, accessible fictional customer case and a structured interview to assess diagnosis, communication, and escalation.

The employer retains any genuine operational or legal requirement and documents which software skills can be taught. It does not assume that removing the degree guarantees a broader qualified pool. During the pilot, it records applications, eligible screens, assessment completion, offers, and supported performance after onboarding. Any apparent improvement is interpreted alongside changes in pay, sourcing, and management support.

The candidate-facing advice is to show relevant examples, explain contributions accurately, and request clarification about permitted tools. The employer-facing advice is to judge the work rather than the polish of the application. Neither side should disclose confidential customer records to make the example seem more impressive.

### Case B: a mid-career technical appointment

A fictional organization seeks an architect for a new integration environment. One applicant has deep experience in a different platform. Instead of treating platform identity as either an automatic rejection or automatic equivalence, the employer separates transferable architecture concepts from product-specific implementation skills. A targeted design discussion uses a synthetic scenario with explicit constraints and asks the candidate to explain tradeoffs and verification methods.

The decision record distinguishes demonstrated architecture judgment, plausible platform adjacency, and unassessed hands-on detail. A development plan is credible only if the employer can supply training and support within the role's delivery commitments. The compensation decision uses the defined job level and supported qualifications rather than the candidate's prior employer brand or previous salary alone.

The case illustrates why a single fit percentage is inadequate. Different uncertainties require different evidence. An additional generic interview would be less useful than a focused check of the remaining essential capability.

### Case C: a multi-state remote role

A fictional employer advertises a remote analytical role across several states. Before publishing, it determines which locations are actually eligible, identifies applicable posting and selection requirements, and chooses a documented compensation geography policy. It then states the work arrangement, range components, assessment process, and accommodation contact.

The employer does not assume that a national posting erases local law. Its legal review distinguishes current requirements from enacted future ones, and its tool inventory distinguishes scheduling automation from a covered consequential assessment. The candidate can compare the actual proposition without guessing whether “remote” means occasional office attendance or unrestricted residence.

The proposed review after hiring examines whether the advertised conditions matched the accepted offer and actual onboarding. A successful case is not merely a filled vacancy; it is a supported decision followed by delivery of the agreed employment conditions.

## 29. Questions, misconceptions, and retrieval answers

Section ID: USH-29. Evidence class: bounded synthesis. Use the referenced sections for detailed answers.

**Does an ATS reject every resume that fails to parse?** No universal rule supports that statement. At least one documented system retains the attached resume when parsing fails. Parsing, configured eligibility rules, ranking, and the employer's decision must be distinguished. See USH-10 and Greenhouse (2026).

**Did structured interviewing begin with AI?** No. Structured assessment predates the current AI wave. Modern research revises estimates and implementation choices; it does not establish that interview structure was invented in the 2020s. See USH-12 and Sackett et al. (2022, 2023).

**Does removing a degree requirement establish skills-first hiring?** No. A change in posting language is one step. Actual assessment, selection, development support, and hiring outcomes must also change. See USH-14 for the underlying study and its limits.

**Is a bias audit a complete approval to use an AI tool?** No. A specific audit requirement has a defined legal scope. It does not automatically prove job-related validity, accessibility, privacy compliance, or compliance in every other jurisdiction. See USH-21 and USH-22.

**Is there one correct U.S. salary for a job title?** No. Job scope, level, labor market, date, pay definition, internal structure, and legitimate qualifications matter. Benchmark data must be interpreted rather than treated as an individualized offer. See USH-17.

**Are the 2031 and 2036 sections predictions of what will definitely happen?** No. They are conditional scenarios without assigned numerical probabilities. They include enabling conditions and signals that would contradict them. See USH-25 and USH-26.

**Can the assistant decide whom to hire from this file alone?** The resource supplies general evidence and process design. It is not a validated tool for autonomous employment decisions and contains no actual candidate dossier. Use it to support accountable, job-related human evaluation, not to infer sensitive traits or manufacture a candidate's qualifications.

**Does a readable PDF establish successful RAG deployment?** No. Text extraction is a format check. A deployed retrieval system needs its own evaluation of indexing, search, answer grounding, source attribution, and boundary handling. See USH-28.

## 30. Glossary

Section ID: USH-30. Evidence class: operational definitions for this reference. These are not replacements for statutory definitions.

**Applicant tracking system (ATS):** software that manages application records and recruitment workflows; specific parsing, screening, ranking, and integration functions vary.

**Assessment:** a method used to gather evidence about a job-relevant attribute or capability. The method's intended construct and use should be explicit.

**Behavioral interview:** an interview approach asking about relevant past behavior. It is not necessarily structured unless questions and evaluation are standardized appropriately.

**Compa-ratio:** salary divided by the adopted range midpoint. It describes placement in a chosen structure, not the validity of that structure.

**Criterion:** the outcome against which an assessment is evaluated, such as a defined measure of job performance. A convenient metric is not automatically an adequate criterion.

**Evidence provenance:** information identifying where a claim came from, when it was observed, and what the source actually supports.

**Job analysis:** inquiry into tasks and required competencies and their relationship. It underpins the design of requirements and assessments.

**Job architecture:** an organization's framework for job families, levels, scope, and related compensation or progression decisions.

**Minimum qualification:** a requirement that must be met under the defined role and applicable rules. It should not be confused with a preference.

**Range penetration:** the distance of salary above a range minimum divided by the range width. Values depend on the chosen boundaries.

**Reliability:** consistency of measurement under relevant conditions. A consistent measure can still measure the wrong thing.

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG):** a design in which retrieved reference material is used to support generated answers. Retrieval quality and answer accuracy require evaluation.

**Scenario:** a conditional account of a plausible future. In this reference a scenario is not an observed fact or a probability-calibrated forecast.

**Skills-first hiring:** selection oriented toward relevant capability, with proxy requirements examined rather than assumed indispensable.

**Structured interview:** a planned interview using consistent job-related questions and evaluation criteria, with appropriate administration and scoring controls.

**Total rewards:** the wider employment proposition, potentially including cash compensation, incentives, benefits, and other elements. Components should not be treated as interchangeable cash.

**Validity:** the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretation and intended use of assessment results. A tool name or vendor assurance does not establish validity.

**Work sample:** a task or simulation resembling relevant work, used to observe capability under specified conditions.
