# Post-COVID Hiring: Workplace Psychology, Sociology, and Selection

**Research edition.** This is a substantial but deliberately **unreleased** foundation for the requested twenty-page-or-longer corpus. It has not passed rendered-page, PDF fidelity, comprehensive source-history reconciliation, independent scholarly review, or hosted GPT retrieval tests. Those gaps must remain visible until genuinely tested. Its 2026 creation date is distinct from the owner-specified © 2025 brand notice.

## PC-001 — Executive interpretation: pandemic acceleration versus universal transformation

The central distinction is between *COVID-19 as an accelerator* and COVID-19 as the unique cause of every development that followed. Remote work, digital applications, freelancing, work–life conflict, automated recruiting and employment inequality existed before 2020. What changed sharply during the public-health crisis was their scale, visibility, and practical legitimacy in work that could be conducted away from a physical site. The subsequent period did not move uniformly toward fully remote work. It produced hybrid experimentation, return-to-office policies, new candidate expectations, uneven occupational access, and a more explicit contest over who controls work location and schedules (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2024; Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology 2024).

For candidates, the selection process increasingly includes digital evidence of competence, competence in digital communication, and opportunities to evaluate organizational flexibility. For employers, the hiring system must distinguish job-related requirements from assumptions inherited from office-centered management. An applicant's video presentation is not the same as the ability to do the job. A working-from-home policy may widen the pool of applicants without ensuring fair promotions after hire. A one-way interview may reduce scheduling effort while shifting costs and stress to applicants. The most useful analysis asks *for whom, under which conditions, at what stage, and on which outcome* a change matters.

The phrase "post-COVID" should be treated as a period descriptor, not a statement that viral disease, its consequences, or its influence ceased on a particular date. This edition compares pre-2020, emergency, early reopening, and later normalization periods, with 2026 evidence separated from the original shock. It does not assume that workers and industries share the same experience.

**Retrieval guardrail:** A user asking "Did COVID make remote work better?" needs an answer about outcome measures and populations, not a universal yes/no. A user asking "Are all interviews virtual now?" should receive a clear no, followed by differences across organizations, industries, occupations and stage.

## PC-002 — Research design and how to read percentages

Hiring statistics are particularly vulnerable to denominator drift. A share of newly posted professional jobs advertised as fully remote is not the share of workers employed remotely, nor is it the share of all vacancies in the economy. A survey of technology-sector knowledge workers says little directly about warehouse staff, drivers, nurses, sales associates or skilled trades. The prevalence of work-from-home depends on definitions: *some telework hours*, *at least one day*, *usually worked from home*, and *fully remote in the reference week* produce different measurements (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2024; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics n.d.; Federal Reserve Board 2026).

Three examples illustrate this problem. Robert Half's Q3 2025 professional-postings dataset categorized 64 percent of new postings as fully on-site, 24 percent as hybrid and 12 percent as fully remote. This is an occupationally bounded dataset of **postings**, not a national census of employees (Robert Half 2025). The Federal Reserve's report on households' 2025 economic well-being found that 39 percent of **workers surveyed** performed at least some work from home in the preceding week, including 17 percent entirely from home. Its population and question differ from Robert Half's postings analysis (Federal Reserve Board 2026). Meanwhile, BLS Current Population Survey telework questions introduced in October 2022 track whether people performed paid work from home during the reference week, with separate hours measures (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics n.d.).

Report these measurements with their own scope and period. Do not average them together. A truthful summary is: "Remote and hybrid work remained significant by 2025, but the measured prevalence differs by survey population, question, and employment stage." Claims of precise universal prevalence are inappropriate without a defined denominator.

**Evidence labels used here:** *observation* means measured within a specified sample; *interpretation* means a reasoned inference; *practice* means recommended behavior; *scenario* means a possible future, not a predicted result. Findings from survey respondents are not automatically causal; longitudinal designs can help, and randomized field experiments are especially valuable when available (Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology 2024).

## PC-003 — Pre-pandemic hiring and the acute transition

Before 2020, telephone screening, applicant tracking systems and online applications were already established, while in-person interactions often remained prominent for final candidate assessment. Some organizations used video interviews and distributed teams, but the onset of COVID-19 meant many employers had to execute recruitment, selection and onboarding without the office visit or travel they had previously expected. This emergency change tested whether practical job evidence could be collected remotely.

The emergency context matters. An individual's pandemic-period work-from-home experience might include illness concerns, closed schools, household crowding, limited equipment and hastily redesigned workflows. Comparing that situation with deliberate, supported remote employment several years later risks attributing emergency stressors to the location itself. Wells and colleagues' systematic review selected 34 studies of remote working during the 2020–2021 period. Many studies had low or very low strength of evidence, while observed harms included reduced physical activity and increased screen time (Wells et al. 2023).

A sound historical chapter therefore separates infrastructure improvements from social change. The ability to sign documents electronically or collaborate online did not guarantee that managers changed how they evaluated autonomy or trust. Interviews conducted over video did not necessarily become more structured or fair simply because questions were asked through a camera. Organizations could preserve informal, impressionistic selection while changing only the delivery channel.

**Practice:** Treat the pandemic transition as a natural historical contrast and a source of organizational lessons, not as a controlled scientific experiment.

## PC-004 — Remote and hybrid work: availability, desirability and who is excluded

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics documented strong differences by industry. In professional, scientific and technical services, the share usually working from home was 16.5 percent in 2019, 46.5 percent in 2021 and 41.4 percent in 2022 under the American Community Survey definition. Information-sector estimates followed a related pattern: 11.4 percent in 2019, 44.8 percent in 2021 and 38.8 percent in 2022. Construction, transportation and hospitality had much lower remote-work rates. Remote eligibility reflects how work is organized, not a universal hierarchy of skill or personal worth (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2024).

The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey indicates that remote work also varies substantially with education. Workers with at least a bachelor's degree were more likely to report fully home-based work than those with a high-school education or less. The result describes distributions of job types and opportunity, and should not be used to judge individual qualifications (Federal Reserve Board 2026).

**Selection consequence:** When posting a role, state whether it is on-site, hybrid, fully remote, location restricted, travel dependent, or subject to in-person onboarding. Separate essential on-site tasks from manager preference. Hybrid does not mean unlimited location independence; employers may impose office attendance, geography, labor-law eligibility, information-security constraints and time-zone coverage.

**Candidate consequence:** Ask for the actual working arrangement before investing in a lengthy application. Define on-site days, location and commute assumptions, travel, core collaboration hours, equipment, data-security obligations and prospects for the policy changing. A job labeled remote can still require travel or residence in specified jurisdictions.

## PC-005 — Productivity, autonomy, collaboration and causal uncertainty

A debate framed as "remote work increases productivity" versus "remote work destroys culture" is too coarse. Productivity can mean tasks completed, revenue, quality, innovation, learning, customer outcomes, costs per unit or employee-reported efficiency. A good research design defines the measure first. Selection also matters: employees who can perform remotely may have different roles, supports, performance histories and preferences from employees assigned on-site.

The Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology's state-of-knowledge review found associations between remote work and higher autonomy and job satisfaction in correlational studies but stressed that correlational survey designs generally do not prove causality. Experimental and quasi-experimental results varied by context, work arrangement and whether studies occurred during the acute pandemic. The review also flagged knowledge transfer and career outcomes as areas requiring more careful interpretation (Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology 2024).

Working remotely can reduce commuting and improve control over certain tasks, but increase deliberate coordination demands. Team members may require explicit documentation, asynchronous norms, access to shared knowledge and planned opportunities for feedback. For novice workers especially, learning by observation may need replacement with intentional mentoring. In fully distributed teams, a productive individual can still be situated in a weak learning system.

**Employer practice:** Use job-specific outcomes, documented work, quality measures and periodic feedback rather than online-status indicators. Evaluate effects separately for new hires, established employees, managers, highly interdependent teams and individual contributors. Avoid interpreting the number of video meetings as a measure of effective collaboration.

## PC-006 — Career progression and proximity bias

Recruitment access is not equivalent to inclusion after hire. A remote position can remove geographic and commuting barriers, while diminished access to informal sponsorship may affect stretch assignments, performance feedback or promotion visibility. "Proximity bias" is the tendency to give greater attention or credibility to people who are physically present or visible to decision-makers, independent of job-related merit. It is a useful organizational risk concept, not a diagnosis of a specific manager.

SIOP's review reported mixed findings about remote work and career consequences. Some remote arrangements support belonging or autonomy, while highly remote workers may face isolation or career uncertainty depending on organizational practices. In the review's discussion of diversity, belonging outcomes differ across individuals and within individuals over time, cautioning against a simple universal inclusion story (Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology 2024).

**Employer practice:** Establish explicit promotion criteria, comparable access to assignments and mentoring, visible written contributions, and documented outcomes for workers regardless of location. Assess whether performance signals disproportionately reward office attendance. Review promotion rates only with attention to role, level, tenure, sample size and privacy safeguards.

**Candidate practice:** Ask how employees in the selected work arrangement receive mentorship, development and leadership exposure, and how promotion decisions distinguish physical visibility from performance.

## PC-007 — Virtual interviews: format is not validity

A virtual interview can be synchronous (live video), asynchronous (recorded answers), panel-based (multiple interviewers), technical (work sample or whiteboard), or primarily text-based. The delivery channel has practical consequences, but selection validity depends on the questions, rubric, standardization, job relation, interviewer training and scoring rules. A physically polished interview setting is not evidence of superior skill.

A 2024 cross-sectional survey in family-medicine residency interviewing found that 68.5 percent of the **54 candidate respondents** reported an easy connection despite the virtual format, while 55.5 percent wanted both in-person and virtual options. Interviewers had their own mixed preferences. These are observations in a particular professional education setting and are not proof that most corporate recruiters prefer video or that virtual interviews are universally standard (Tolentino et al. 2024).

Video communication introduces variables such as internet reliability, camera framing, background noise, the difficulty of turn-taking, gaze alignment and accessibility. An asynchronous interview adds concerns about one-sided communication, repeatability, instructions and whether applicants can ask questions. Technical inequities should not be silently transformed into competence judgments.

**Candidate practice:** Confirm the platform and time zone, test audio, prepare quiet conditions where possible, ask about backup channels, structure job-related examples around context/action/result, and communicate promptly when an accommodation or alternate modality is needed. A technical glitch should be handled calmly without assuming it proves a lack of ability.

**Employer practice:** Explain duration, stages, accommodations, data retention and assessment criteria; use the same essential questions and scoring rubric; allow candidates substantive time to ask questions; establish a fair process for connection failures.

## PC-008 — Candidate psychology: autonomy and uncertainty together

Applicants interpret the hiring process as information about the likely employment relationship. Timely communication, clear expectations and fair opportunities to demonstrate competence can build trust; opaque delays, unnecessary hurdles and generic rejection messages can undermine it. Remote hiring reduces travel and permits more geographic access, but may also create distance from the organization's human context.

The sociological significance extends beyond convenience. An applicant living outside a concentrated metropolitan labor market may see newly reachable opportunities. A caregiver may benefit from less travel. Yet a highly visible remote vacancy may attract a much larger field of competitors, making the experience feel more impersonal. These effects can coexist; counting applications alone cannot establish well-being or equity.

The 2021 Great Resignation illustrates why workers' motivations should not be reduced to a single slogan. Pew's 2022 U.S. survey of workers who voluntarily left a job in 2021 found 63 percent cited low pay, 63 percent cited lack of advancement, and 57 percent cited feeling disrespected, among other reasons. This is evidence about respondents' stated motivations, not a randomized attribution of quits to the pandemic or a forecast of current workers' decisions (Parker and Horowitz 2022).

**Practice:** Encourage candidates to treat an employer's selection process as two-way information gathering. Maintain a dated application log, prepare a few strong evidence-backed examples, ask for process timelines and role expectations, and avoid letting a single rejection stand in for an overall assessment of competence. Employers should use transparent checkpoints and give process updates proportionate to the stage.

## PC-009 — Mental health at work: work design before individual blame

The language of workplace mental health should distinguish mental health conditions, emotional distress, burnout, job insecurity, social isolation and ordinary fatigue. These are not interchangeable categories. Burnout is an occupational phenomenon associated with chronic workplace stress, not a diagnosis a recruiter should assign to candidates or employees based on demeanor.

The World Health Organization identifies psychosocial risks including high workload, lack of autonomy, harassment, unclear roles, insufficient colleague support, job insecurity and conflicting work–home demands. WHO estimates depression and anxiety cost approximately twelve billion workdays and US$1 trillion in lost productivity annually worldwide. The estimate does not establish that remote work caused those outcomes (World Health Organization n.d.).

Mind Share Partners' 2025 U.S. workplace survey, conducted with Qualtrics among 1,153 employed full-time adults, reported that about half experienced moderate-to-severe burnout, depression or anxiety, while fear of job consequences for discussing mental health persisted. These are self-reported survey findings, not clinical prevalence estimates of the entire labor force. Their value lies in showing that well-being, trust and workplace stigma remain relevant questions for managers (Mind Share Partners 2025).

Remote work may provide flexibility but make boundaries harder to maintain. Someone can benefit from removing a commute yet feel isolated; another person can benefit from social contact in the office yet experience more commute or caregiving stress. Prescriptions should therefore target workload, predictability, support, autonomy, accessible communication, and time for recovery rather than assume one location policy heals everyone.

**Employer practice:** Evaluate job design and workload with workers, train managers to respond supportively, respect confidential disclosure rules, provide reasonable accommodations where applicable, and distinguish appropriate performance feedback from personal medical judgments. **Candidate practice:** Assess benefits and scheduling policies without feeling obliged to share medical history during selection. This corpus gives general workplace information, not medical advice.

## PC-010 — Inclusion, disability access and the double edge of remote eligibility

Remote work can expand opportunity when geography, commuting, mobility, household responsibilities or other barriers previously limited participation. It can also shift inequality into other areas: broadband access, private home workspace, opportunities for mentorship, access to equipment, job-site technology and promotion visibility.

Wharton researchers David Hsu and Prasanna Tambe analyzed a large set of STEM startup job listings from 2018–2022 and found that postings shifting from on-site to remote attracted 15 percent more female applicants and 33 percent more underrepresented minority applicants. The figures measure **changes in applicant pools for particular jobs**; they do not show that hiring decisions or advancement became fairer in every organization (Basiouny 2024).

An inclusive process should distinguish access to apply, participation in assessment, evaluation consistency, offer rates, onboarding, retention and advancement. The same job posting can be accessible in one dimension and inaccessible in another. For example, removing geographic requirements may increase applications while mandatory uncaptioned video interviews disadvantage some participants. A disability accommodation can be a job-access requirement, not a special bonus in a hiring competition.

**Employer practice:** Provide accessible application channels, clear essential requirements, alternative communication methods, consistent rubrics, captions when needed, and an explained accommodation path. Validate AI-supported tools against relevant accessibility and fairness concerns; do not claim that an algorithm is unbiased because a vendor advertises it as objective.

## PC-011 — Freelancing, gig work and security: definitions before forecasts

"Freelancer," "independent professional," "platform gig worker," "temporary employee," "contractor," and "self-employed worker" describe overlapping but nonidentical groups. Someone doing one side project, someone operating a full-time consultancy, a rideshare driver and a temporary employee may all be classified differently across surveys or laws. Therefore declarations that "half of all workers will be freelancers" are risky unless the underlying survey defines who counts and whether the number is an estimate or projection.

The pandemic period accelerated online contracting for some occupations while also exposing platform workers and contingent employees to insecurity and work-site risks. Flexibility can mean control over clients and schedules; insecurity can mean variable income, business expenses, lack of paid leave and dependence on a small number of purchasers. Multiple clients can spread revenue risk but are not an automatic substitute for benefits or predictable demand.

Upwork's research demonstrates sizable skilled freelance markets but is based on particular definitions and commissioned research. Its measures should not be treated as national Census counts or used to declare permanent employment obsolete (Upwork Research Institute 2025).

**Candidate practice:** Compare expected annual net compensation after taxes, insurance, unpaid selling/admin time, work gaps, equipment, learning and retirement contributions. Ask about contract scope, payment terms, confidentiality, ownership of deliverables and end conditions. **Employer practice:** Classify workers based on actual work and applicable rules, not merely what a vendor calls a contract; provide clear onboarding and security controls.

## PC-012 — Great Resignation, Big Stay and the lifecycle of labor-market labels

The Great Resignation refers to historically high voluntary quits and job changes in the U.S. labor-market recovery, especially 2021–2022. The label is useful shorthand but should not be mistaken for a monocausal theory. Many workers moved to other jobs rather than leaving paid work permanently. Pandemic reassessment, better offers, pay, management experiences and sector-specific demand all contributed to different worker decisions (Parker and Horowitz 2022).

"The Big Stay" describes a later slowdown in voluntary turnover and greater reluctance to switch jobs. A 2024 CIPD-based article reported this language in the **UK**, with employers anticipating reduced vacancies and more employees staying. Its quoted 55 percent represents employers expecting to maintain staffing, **not** 55 percent of U.S. workers deciding not to quit. Importing that figure into U.S. labor statistics would be a category error (Jackson 2024).

As market conditions evolve, the same employee may value autonomy, stability, development and compensation simultaneously. A tight labor market can increase leverage for some workers, while recession concerns or hiring freezes make others more cautious. Analysis should include quit rates, new hires, job openings, labor-force participation and industry segmentation rather than rely only on trend labels.

**Practice:** Date each label and use it to introduce verified statistics, not replace them. Avoid interpreting "quiet quitting" as evidence of a psychological disorder or a fact about an individual employee.

## PC-013 — Generational patterns without stereotypes

Generational categories can describe survey differences, but they conflate age, career stage, family responsibilities, industry composition and cohort effects. A 25-year-old new hire may want in-person mentoring, while a different 25-year-old in a remote-friendly field might prefer geographic mobility. A senior worker may value remote autonomy but also need intentional sponsor relationships. Employer programs should adapt to demonstrated needs rather than assume all Gen Z or Gen X workers behave identically.

Gallup reported that fully remote work was the first choice for a smaller share of remote-capable Gen Z employees than for older comparison groups in one survey. The value of the finding is a question for policy design, not a license to attribute individual preferences to age (Gallup 2025). A methodologically sound recommendation is to make mentoring, development and collaborative presence possible without forcing a single arrangement on every worker or team.

Ask: Does this role require apprenticeship, hands-on observation or group problem solving? How are those needs supported across arrangements? Does the employee have genuine choice? Are supervisors trained to evaluate contribution equitably?

## PC-014 — Skills-first hiring, ATS and AI: avoid folklore

The shift toward competency evidence is not the same as a universal abandonment of degrees. Changing a job advertisement's degree language does not prove that employees without degrees have equal chances of being hired or promoted. An ATS may store applications, parse documents, route workflows, rank candidates or support recruiter review depending on its configuration. There is no universal ATS rule that an unconventional font or résumé layout automatically triggers rejection.

Structured interviews and work samples, when designed around job requirements and applied consistently, can provide clearer evidence than informal affinity judgments. The design and administration of a test matter more than whether it is online. Employers should define job-essential competence before selecting tools, then document the relationship between observed behavior and that competence.

AI tools may assist in extracting application information, drafting communications, surfacing potential matches or supporting assessment. But claims that algorithms read body language accurately or eliminate bias are not supported merely by the existence of commercial products. Automated scoring raises privacy, accessibility and disparate-impact concerns. Current jurisdictional requirements vary and can change quickly; consult current official legal text before operational use. The supplementary 2026 research for this draft includes primary labor datasets but **does not certify a fifty-state AI-hiring compliance inventory**.

**Candidate practice:** Tell the truth about qualifications and outcomes, use AI to prepare and edit without inventing credentials or clients, and ask what evidence will be evaluated. **Employer practice:** Require vendor documentation, maintain human accountability, test accessibility and disparate effects appropriately, and record data-retention and candidate-notice obligations.

## PC-015 — Employer design: a psychologically credible selection journey

A well-designed hiring journey has explicit stages: job analysis, accessible announcement, intake, initial screening, structured assessment, interview, reference or background checks where applicable, selection decision, communication and onboarding. The exact steps vary by jurisdiction and role. The central requirement is coherence: applicants should know what evidence is requested and employers should know why it matters.

A selection panel should agree in advance on the assessment dimensions. For distributed work, dimensions might include written communication, documentation habits, asynchronous coordination, information security and autonomy when truly job-related. They should not be interpreted as personality proxies. Each interviewer should document evidence against the rubric rather than general impressions such as "seems like a culture fit."

Candidate experience is an operational outcome. Measure time from application to initial response, number of assessment stages, total candidate time requested, offer acceptance, withdrawals, accommodation requests handled, and satisfaction where data permits. Benchmark measures against job family, seniority, selection validity and fairness. Do not optimize only for low screening cost while externalizing excessive unpaid work to applicants.

Retention begins before the start date: accurate job descriptions, candid communication about hybrid policy, realistic workload expectations, clear resources for remote onboarding, and specific mentorship plans establish whether the psychological contract presented in recruiting is credible.

## PC-016 — Candidate decision guide and interview questions

A candidate evaluating a remote or hybrid role can ask what outcome is expected in the first 90 days, which work requires shared hours, how decisions and specifications are documented, and whether promotion criteria are the same across office and remote locations. Ask how new hires receive feedback, who makes selection decisions, whether the interview requires an assessment and how much time it will take. For a contract role, ask about project funding, extension dependencies, ownership, expenses and payment schedule.

A useful method is to maintain an evidence ledger for each opportunity: (1) stated requirement, (2) source, (3) date, (4) whether independently verified, (5) unanswered question, (6) actual fit evidence, (7) conditional risk. This is a decision aid, not a personality score. Keep private compensation and medical matters out of public notes.

When discussing adaptability, use examples based on verifiable achievements: describe a particular disruption, what you changed, how communication or outcomes improved and what remains uncertain. If a measure was not recorded, do not manufacture a percentage. A precise qualitative outcome is stronger than a fictitious quantitative accomplishment.

## PC-017 — Employer action framework and review measures

Employers should first identify which activities require physical presence and which do not. Then they can design fair role-specific options. A distributed team benefits from clear documentation, synchronous meeting purposes, reliable tool access, mentorship, performance criteria and structured feedback.

Measure recruiting outcomes and post-hire outcomes separately. Recruitment diversity describes who applies and advances, while employee inclusion describes access to resources, safety, belonging, assignments and progression. Health benefits may be available while actual workload still drives exhaustion. An inclusive statement in a job ad does not prove experience once hired.

When changing remote policies, examine employee distribution, operating risks, attrition and customer outcomes, not only headcount in buildings. Pilot changes by team where possible and specify what evidence would trigger reversal. An analytically mature employer admits that some roles benefit more from shared physical presence than others, and that many on-site workers need flexibility in scheduling even when location flexibility is impossible.

## PC-018 — Outlook: conditional scenarios and falsification signals

**Scenario A, sustained hybrid equilibrium:** Professional organizations retain a mix of shared in-person time and remote focus. This requires investment in documentation, collaboration processes, equitable performance reviews and manager training. Falsification signal: sustained evidence that hybrid offers and employee participation collapse across several comparable datasets.

**Scenario B, stronger return-to-office policies:** A larger share of employers demands attendance despite worker preferences. The effect depends on labor-market conditions and how job requirements are justified. Falsification signal: comparable postings and worker datasets showing no material reversal in remote eligibility.

**Scenario C, labor-market and AI restructuring:** Employers use more automated workflow assistance and structured competence evidence, while regulatory and fairness scrutiny increases. No fixed adoption figure or prediction of worker displacement is asserted. Falsification signal: measured hiring outcomes and tool adoption do not support anticipated efficiencies or fairer selection.

**Scenario D, diversified careers:** More workers combine employment, contracts and personal projects as market conditions permit. This does not imply that all gig work is secure or that stable employment disappears. Falsification signal: consistent longitudinal evidence of contracting participation decline under comparable definitions.

Future research should distinguish changes arising from economic cycles, organizational strategy, digital technology and the pandemic's long aftermath. Avoid attributing everything after 2020 to COVID-19.

## PC-019 — Lexicon and atomic glossary for retrieval

Each entry is an editorial, general-domain definition; it does **not** prove that a particular person uses the term.

### PC-G001 — Remote-capable role
Definition: A job whose essential duties can be performed away from the employer's location for some or all working time. Nonexample: work performed exclusively on-site because essential tasks require physical presence. Related: PC-G002, PC-G003.

### PC-G002 — Fully remote work
Definition: A work arrangement in which all ordinary workdays are conducted away from the employer's site, subject to explicitly disclosed travel or location restrictions. Not equivalent to "any work from home." Related: PC-G001, PC-G003.

### PC-G003 — Hybrid work
Definition: A work arrangement combining remote and on-site days or activities. Its scheduling and location rules must be specified. Related: PC-G001, PC-G002.

### PC-G004 — Asynchronous interview
Definition: An interview step in which responses are recorded or submitted for later review rather than exchanged live. It may be video, audio or written. Related: PC-G005.

### PC-G005 — Structured interview
Definition: A job-related assessment using consistent substantive questions and predetermined evaluation criteria. It can be conducted in person or virtually. Related: PC-G004, PC-G015.

### PC-G006 — Candidate experience
Definition: The practical and perceived quality of interactions across application, assessment, communication and decision stages. Related: PC-G004, PC-G005.

### PC-G007 — Proximity bias
Definition: A tendency to favor people who are physically nearby or more visible in the workplace, beyond job-related evidence. Related: PC-G003, PC-G008.

### PC-G008 — Psychological safety
Definition: A team's shared sense that interpersonal risks such as asking questions and acknowledging mistakes can be taken without inappropriate humiliation or retaliation. Not a guarantee against all performance consequences. Related: PC-G007, PC-G009.

### PC-G009 — Burnout
Definition: An occupational phenomenon associated with chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed; not an interchangeable synonym for depression or a diagnosis made by an interview platform. Related: PC-G008.

### PC-G010 — Work–life boundary
Definition: A chosen or imposed separation between working obligations and personal time, location or attention. Remote work can strengthen or weaken boundaries depending on design. Related: PC-G003, PC-G009.

### PC-G011 — Gig work
Definition: Paid work involving discrete tasks or short engagements, sometimes mediated by platforms; definitions vary by study and jurisdiction. Not synonymous with all freelancing. Related: PC-G012.

### PC-G012 — Independent professional
Definition: A person providing professional services under independently managed client relationships, subject to applicable employment and tax classification rules. Related: PC-G011.

### PC-G013 — Great Resignation
Definition: Informal label for a period of elevated voluntary quits and labor mobility, especially in the U.S. during 2021–2022. Related: PC-G014.

### PC-G014 — Big Stay
Definition: Informal label for periods of relatively low quits or employee reluctance to change employers; not a standardized statistical series. Related: PC-G013.

### PC-G015 — Skills-based hiring
Definition: Selection emphasizing demonstrated job-relevant competencies rather than relying exclusively on credentials or pedigree. A changed job advertisement alone does not establish equitable implementation. Related: PC-G005, PC-G016.

### PC-G016 — Applicant tracking system (ATS)
Definition: Software for managing applicants, recruitment data and hiring workflows; capabilities vary widely by vendor and configuration. It is not necessarily a universal automatic rejector. Related: PC-G015.

### PC-G017 — Automated employment decision tool
Definition: Technology that automates or materially supports evaluation or selection under a specified operational or legal definition; jurisdictional rules may differ. Related: PC-G018.

### PC-G018 — Algorithmic bias
Definition: Systematic unfairness or differential outcomes associated with data, design, evaluation or deployment of an algorithm. It requires context-specific measurement and cannot be ruled out by vendor claims alone. Related: PC-G017.

### PC-G019 — Reasonable accommodation
Definition: An adjustment to enable equal participation or performance where applicable under governing law; not an optional favor or an assumption about a protected characteristic. Related: PC-G006.

### PC-G020 — Candidate ghosting
Definition: A hiring participant's unexplained cessation of communication after meaningful interaction. Frequency and cause should not be inferred without defined data. Related: PC-G006.

## PC-020 — Voice and response conventions

AskJamie™ domain tone: calm, direct, analytical, context-sensitive and practical. Use exact dates and denominators when discussing percentages. Ask "What population? Which period? Compared with what?" before promoting research findings to decisions. Avoid dramatic verbs ("revolutionized," "obliterated," "everyone now") unless plainly supported. Explain terms such as "proximity bias" before assuming familiarity. Distinguish what is observed, plausible, recommended and unresolved.

For candidate-facing answers, give a short factual orientation and actionable questions. For employer-facing answers, give decision criteria and measures. For technical/RAG answers, state the section ID, definition, scope, citations and stale-claim warning. For explanatory psychology, do not diagnose job seekers or stigmatize demographic groups. All illustrative examples must say they are illustrative and must never claim real outcomes or candidates.

This domain language layer is NOT an extracted private personal voice profile. A separately governed AJ01 private voice toolkit owns observed vocabulary and personal phrasing. This corpus's glossary is about labor-market concepts only.
